# Solving the Rubik's Cube with Approximate Policy Iteration

> **TL;DR:** Autodidactic Iteration (ADI) generates training states by scrambling a known goal, constructs one-step value/policy targets with the current network and learns without human solution demonstrations. DeepCube then combines that network with asynchronous MCTS and a shortest-path pass over an explored graph. The reported complete solve rate is for a finite cube test set, not a universal or shortest-solution guarantee.

## Motivation

Random play is unlikely to reach the single solved Rubik's Cube state, making ordinary sparse-reward policy iteration uninformative. Goal-originating random walks ensure that some training states remain close enough to obtain a useful reward and let learned value information propagate to harder states. The method assumes a known goal and deterministic forward/backward puzzle dynamics (Sections 1, 3-4).

## Contributions

ADI is an iterative bootstrapping procedure for a joint value/policy network. Its distinctive training-state distribution replaces reliance on successful random full episodes or human solution labels. The resulting DeepCube planner uses the learned value and action prior to focus MCTS, then shortens a found solution through graph search.

The complete scientific copies read here demonstrate Rubik's Cube only. They do not supply a 15-puzzle experiment, despite a broader claim in one author webpage abstract. The previous 15-puzzle tag is therefore removed from the evidence-backed task list. No common checkpoint across multiple puzzle domains is established; the entry remains a specialist constructive planner.

## Methodology

Cube states use a 20-by-24 cubie-location encoding, and actions are twelve quarter-turns. Reward is +1 for reaching the goal and -1 otherwise. Each training iteration scrambles the solved cube, expands all one-step successors of each sampled state and evaluates them with the current network. The maximum reward-plus-estimated-value supplies a value target, and its maximizing action supplies a policy target. A mean-squared value loss and policy cross entropy train the network with RMSProp (Algorithm 1).

Samples receive weight 1/D, where D is the number of moves in their generating scramble, rather than the unknown optimal distance. This emphasizes states nearer the goal distribution and empirically reduces instability. The conference copy explicitly states that nonlinear approximation and TD learning provide no theoretical convergence guarantee here. “No human demonstrations” does not mean no supplied environment structure, state encoding, goal test or search.

At inference, asynchronous workers share an MCTS tree, using policy priors, exploration and virtual loss. Backups retain the maximum encountered value rather than averaging returns. After a goal is found, the explored structure is converted to an undirected graph, expanded with children in the conference description, and searched by BFS for a shorter path. Reversibility makes reverse graph edges usable. The shortest path in this restricted graph need not be the globally shortest cube solution. The feedforward network has shared 4,096/2,048 layers and separate 512-unit policy/value branches; the outputs are a twelve-way policy and scalar value (Appendix B).

## Experiments

The conference-marked copy reports two million training iterations, approximately eight billion encountered states including repetitions and 44 hours on a 32-core Xeon E5-2620 server with three Titan XP GPUs. Its parallel inference uses 32 workers with batched neural requests (Appendix B). Training count, large search cost and the absence of human labels are separate facts.

On 640 cubes generated by 1,000 random moves from the goal, both DeepCube and Kociemba solve every tested case within one hour. The paper reports median DeepCube length 30 and matching or beating Kociemba's length on 55% of cases; Kociemba is much faster. The BFS post-processing reduces the median by three moves relative to taking the initial MCTS path. These are finite random-scramble results, not an exhaustive state-space test or a guarantee of uniform random-state sampling (Section 5).

A separate 100-cube, fifteen-scramble comparison reports median length 13 and agreement with the Korf reference solution on 74% of cases. The text itself notes that its Korf implementation uses a half-turn metric while the paper uses a quarter-turn metric, so the comparison should not be promoted to a consistent universal optimality certificate. The conference copy reports 7,823 average expanded nodes and a maximum of 24,175; the older arXiv v1 reports 1,136/3,425, and these version-specific counts are not interchangeable.

Appendix A observes frequent move-conjugation patterns and corner/edge grouping in generated solution strings. This is a qualitative post-hoc analysis of this solver's paths, not evidence of human-like reasoning in general or verified transfer to protein folding, robotics or other puzzles.

## Limitations

The method relies on reversible model-known dynamics, a supplied goal and affordable successor generation. Goal-scramble length is only a training proxy for distance; long random walks can contain cancellations. Nonlinear bootstrapping stability is empirical, and the network value is not an admissible heuristic. Learned search may find suboptimal paths, and graph post-processing only improves paths within the constructed graph. Runtime, node counts, metric conventions and solver implementations must be matched before claiming an efficiency or optimality advantage.

### Curator Notes

The author-hosted conference PDF contains unresolved timing differences: Section 5 repeats a ten-minute median, its page-8 histogram uses seconds and shows a median on the order of tens of seconds, and Appendix C lists 40 seconds for DeepCube. The appendix also gives node/rate entries that cannot all be interpreted as a single consistent arithmetic throughput measurement. These values are recorded as a reporting/configuration inconsistency rather than combined into a precise speedup. The older arXiv histogram uses minutes. The clearly supported conclusion is finite-test solving and useful path quality, with substantial implementation/version dependence.
