# Learning Cut Selection for Mixed-Integer Linear Programming via Hierarchical Sequence Model

> **TL;DR:** A high-level policy chooses a cut ratio and a pointer network selects that many cuts in order. HEM learns a root-node cut-selection heuristic inside SCIP; its policy-gradient identity is not an optimization or convergence guarantee for the learned solver policy.

## Motivation

Adding every valid cut can burden LP solves. Selecting cuts requires deciding which, how many and in what order; separate per-cut scoring can miss interactions. Controlled permutations of the same selected cuts change measured solver performance, motivating a sequence policy. This is sensitivity of a numerical solver's procedure, not a change to the mathematical feasible set caused solely by reordering identical inequalities (Sections 1–3).

## Contributions

- A hierarchical cut policy combines a continuous ratio decision with autoregressive selection of an ordered subset.
- A score-function gradient trains both levels using downstream SCIP feedback.
- Per-dataset experiments cover synthetic MILPs, selected MIPLIB subsets, industrial-style benchmarks and production planning, including transfer to larger instances and longer solver budgets.

## Methodology

**Solver interface.** The candidate pool contains cuts generated by SCIP. A state is represented by thirteen features per cut: coefficient/objective statistics, parallelism, efficacy, support, integral support and normalized violation. Learned selection changes which valid candidates enter the relaxation; it does not generate arbitrary new inequalities. Preservation of integer-feasible solutions relies on the host's valid cut generation and branch-and-cut machinery, not a learned correctness theorem (Sections 2/4; Appendix F.1).

**Two policies.** An LSTM encoder with 128-dimensional hidden state and two-layer MLP predicts Gaussian parameters. Transforming a Gaussian sample by (tanh(K)+1)/2 yields ratio k; the chosen length is floor(Nk). A pointer-network LSTM encoder/decoder selects distinct candidate indices sequentially, modeling dependence on prior selections. It can handle variable input/output lengths, but its sequence representation is not an asserted permutation-invariant encoding (Section 4; Appendices F.3–F.4).

**What the gradient proves.** Proposition 1 and Appendix A differentiate a mixture of ratio and conditional sequence policies for an expectation under a fixed initial-state distribution. With differentiable normalized policy densities, appropriate interchange of differentiation/integration and parameter-independent reward, the usual log-likelihood identities give gradients for both levels. This supplies an estimator for the stated contextual objective; it does not prove global policy optimality, neural convergence or a guaranteed solver speedup. Main experiments use one root separation round, so the cut-selection episode is a contextual bandit rather than a demonstrated multiround learned MDP solution (Sections 4–5).

**Training choices.** Adam learning rates are 10⁻⁴ for the lower policy and 5×10⁻⁴ for the upper policy. Each of 100 epochs collects 32 samples; the upper policy updates once per two lower-policy updates. Easy tasks optimize negative solving time, medium/hard tasks negative 300-second primal-dual integral. Production planning uses a dual-bound-change objective in the main results and a separate 600-second integral variant in the appendix. The paper calls the former “negative dual bound improvement”; the released code stores old LP objective minus new LP objective as a cost, so sign conventions should be checked against the actual loss rather than copied in isolation. Two-timescale convergence results cited for linear settings do not establish convergence of these neural policies (Appendix F.3).

## Experiments

**Protocol.** SCIP 8.0.0 generates/selects cuts only at the root, for one round; other advertised features including presolve and heuristics remain enabled. The default comparison therefore uses this restricted cutting schedule, not unrestricted default SCIP at every node. Most runs have a 300-second limit; production planning separately uses 600 seconds and long-budget experiments three hours. Results average solver seeds 1/2/3. Hardware is reported as eight 3090 Ti GPUs and two Intel Gold 6246R CPUs; this describes the available machine, not necessarily utilization by every test (Section 5; Appendix F.3).

**Training/test scope.** Easy set-cover, MIS and multiple-knapsack tasks use 10,000 training and 100 held-out test instances according to Appendix D.1, which is more specific than the main text's blanket 80/20 split. Other datasets use 80/20 splits and model selection on the training set, without a separate validation set. MIPLIB results concern two feature-similarity-selected subsets of twenty and forty instances, after removing numerical/infeasible/unsuitable cases; their test splits are correspondingly small. This is not a full-MIPLIB generalist evaluation. Models and rewards are trained per dataset; unchanged-model transfer is within task across size/budget (Section 5; Appendix D).

**Metric definitions and denominators.** “Improvement” in tables is relative to NoCuts: (M(NoCuts)−M(method))/M(NoCuts), using time for easy tasks and integral for harder tasks. It is not automatically a gain over Default or SBP. The main paper describes PD integral as area between primal/dual bounds, while the released environment obtains SCIP's `getPrimalDualIntegral()` and `getGap()` statistics; exact solver normalization/initialization must be preserved rather than treating values as raw objective-unit areas or optimal-solution percentages. Time-limit ties, large sentinel gaps when incumbents are absent and rounded zero gaps need their solver context (Section 5; Appendix C.1/G.2; official environment).

**Main results.** HEM has the best reported PD integral across the nine main Table 1 datasets and generally improves easy-task time. MIS time is 1.76 seconds versus Default 3.88 and SBP 2.43. CORLAT integral is 1,079.99 versus Default 2,412.09. On Load Balancing all methods hit about 300 seconds: HEM integral 9,496.42 slightly improves Default 9,589.19, without establishing that the instances were solved. HEM does not win every metric: on mixed supportcase NV time 161.67 is slightly better than HEM 162.96, despite HEM's better integral. The paper's “up to 16.4%” time comparison should not conflate differences in NoCuts-relative improvement percentages with a direct SBP-relative runtime reduction (Tables 1–3).

**Baselines and ablations.** Human rules include NoCuts, Default, random, normalized violation and efficacy; nondefault score-based rules retain 20%. SBP is an adapted MLP/evolution-strategy implementation motivated by earlier cut selectors, not a rerun of every original paper's algorithm. HEM without the upper level uses an end token and can still choose output length; HEM-ratio uses a fixed length; HEM-ratio-order additionally restores candidate order. Full hierarchy helps many cases but has exceptions: end-token HEM slightly beats full HEM on set-cover time/integral and Load-Balancing integral. Order ablations also have mixed individual metrics, so all-component dominance is unsupported (Appendices F.2/F.4/G.3; Tables 10–11).

**Transfer, time and objective trade-offs.** MIS transfer labels “4×/9×” and set-cover “2×/4×” refer to products of variable/constraint dimensions, not those factors in node count alone. HEM improves the listed larger-instance tables. Training for 300 seconds and testing for three hours retains lower integral on both MIPLIB subsets, but mixed-supportcase time is 3,465.48 seconds versus SBP 3,014.63, NV 2,561.10 and random 2,605.78. Better bound progress and faster completion are distinct objectives (Tables 4/13/14).

**Overhead and qualitative evidence.** Average inference time ranges from 0.09 to 1.58 seconds; set-cover inference is material compared with its reported 1.85-second solve time, so an amortized end-to-end accounting should retain these components. PCA plots on selected instances illustrate diversity but do not prove cut complementarity or a general causal performance benefit. Training curves show mean/std over three seeds and are smoothed, not a convergence theorem (Table 6; Figures 3/7/8).

## Limitations

### Discussed in the Paper

Learning sequence policies on challenging instances can be unstable or trapped in poor local solutions; fixed-ratio sequence variants sometimes lose to simpler scoring policies. The authors use selected MIPLIB subsets because learning the heterogeneous full benchmark is difficult. Reward choice can favor integral improvement while worsening completion time; better features and hyperparameter/validation choices may improve results (Appendices F.1/G.3/G.6).

### Curator Notes

HEM is specialist learned cut management with separate dataset training. The formal MILP interface and multiple benchmark families do not demonstrate a shared unchanged cross-task model. Claims should retain the one-round root restriction, screened small MIPLIB splits, train-set checkpoint selection, scoring-baseline adaptation, inference cost and metric-specific negative cases. Host solver validity and eventual exactness under its usual assumptions must not be attributed to the policy-gradient derivation.
