# Learning to Optimize Permutation Flow Shop Scheduling via Graph-Based Imitation Learning

> **TL;DR:** PFSS-IL imitates NEH-generated job orders with a gated graph encoder and an autoregressive attention decoder. In the six synthetic five-machine settings, its average gap to NEH is about 1.3%, versus 6.8% for the compared actor–critic model. These are expert-relative gaps; the model is not an exact PFSS solver, and its benchmark gaps are larger.

## Motivation

Permutation flow shop scheduling seeks one common job permutation for a fixed sequence of machines, minimizing makespan. Each job visits every machine once in that order, machines process one job at a time, and operations are nonpreemptive. Reinforcement learning can require lengthy exploration; high-quality heuristic schedules provide a direct supervised signal. The paper uses an NEH variant as teacher and graph relations between processing-time vectors as an alternative to an LSTM encoder. [pp. 1–3]

## Contributions

- Trains a constructive PFSS policy through expert-driven behavior cloning.
- Embeds jobs and pairwise processing-profile differences with gated graph convolutions.
- Demonstrates transfer from twenty-job training instances to as many as 1,000 jobs, keeping the machine dimension fixed.
- Supplies ablations, normal-distribution/machine-count experiments, a small MIP-versus-NEH comparison and a separately supervised TSP adaptation. [pp. 2–12]

## Methodology

Each job is a node whose feature vector contains its processing times on all m machines. Pairwise Euclidean distances in this feature space initialize edge features. The graph retains approximately the nearest twenty percent of nodes per job. GGCN layers update node and edge embeddings through residual, normalized and gated aggregation. The graph is a learned representation of processing-profile relationships, rather than a machine-resource feasibility graph. [pp. 3–4, Eq. 1]

The decoder combines mean graph embedding with the first and latest selected job embeddings. Eight-head attention refines the context; a final clipped compatibility score and mask produce a distribution over unscheduled jobs. Greedy inference chooses the highest-probability remaining job until a complete permutation is built. Masking ensures each job appears once; the standard flow-shop simulation determines the resulting makespan. [pp. 3–5, Eqs. 2–5]

Training minimizes cross-entropy on teacher state–action sequences generated by the NEH heuristic. Exact optimization is considered as a potential teacher but is not used for the main training set because of its cost. The released supervised path uses teacher forcing and negative log-likelihood; it is distinct from an included RL training path. Expert quality, teacher-induced state distribution and ties in expert orders therefore matter. [pp. 5, 10; train.py and nets/attention_model.py]

**Classification: Specialist / constructive.** Job-count transfer reuses parameters because the embedding input dimension depends on machine count m, not number of jobs n. The paper explicitly requires m to remain consistent between training and testing. Different machine counts/distributions use separately trained settings. Appendix G trains a two-dimensional TSP adaptation with Gurobi solutions as teacher, rather than applying the PFSS checkpoint unchanged; it is architecture reuse, not a shared cross-native solver. [pp. 5–6, 10–12]

## Experiments

Default training uses 12,800 twenty-job, five-machine instances, 1,000 validation instances, batch size 128 and fifty epochs, with learning rate 1e−4 decaying by 0.96 per epoch. The primary synthetic distribution is Gamma(shape=1, scale=2); the normal-distribution experiment clips negative values to zero and trains its own model. Tests contain 1,000 instances for 20/50/100 jobs and 100 for 200/500/1000 jobs. Taillard/VRF benchmark sizes have ten instances each; separate five- and twenty-machine models are used. Runs average three seeds. Hardware is a Xeon Silver 4214 2.20 GHz ×48 CPU configuration and RTX 2080 Ti on Ubuntu 20.04. [pp. 5–6, 10–12]

Table 1's six Gamma/five-machine gaps to NEH are 3.4%, 1.9%, 0.8%, 0.4%, 0.6% and 0.7% for PFSS-IL, averaging approximately 1.3%. The compared RL gaps average approximately 6.8%; the reduction is about 5.5 percentage points. At 1,000 jobs, PFSS-IL has makespan 1055.5 versus RL's 1138.9 and NEH's 1048.2. The reported 89.9-second IL, 81.8-second RL and 43.1-hour NEH times are sums over the 100-instance test set, not per-instance latency. [pp. 5–7]

This result does not mean PFSS-IL is the most accurate method. NEH supplies the zero-gap reference and generally has better makespans; iterated greedy also beats IL in several small settings. IL inference is a little slower than the RL baseline throughout the main comparison. On Taillard, IL's gaps range from 9.4% to 12.8%; on VRF they range from 8.2% to 15.9%. At Taillard (100,20), IL and RL both report 12.5%. These benchmark results should not be summarized as a universal 1.3% gap or uniform strict improvement. [pp. 5–6]

Table 5 separates architecture and training effects. IL/GGCN has 365k parameters and 12.4 seconds per epoch; RL/LSTM has a 709k actor plus 267k critic and 31.0 seconds per epoch. The headline 37% compares total training networks (365/976), not the size ratio against the actor alone at deployment. Table 4 accounts separately for 165.1 seconds of expert generation and 620 seconds of IL training versus 1550 seconds of RL training. For small one-off workloads, direct NEH remains cheaper overall. [pp. 6–7]

The normal-distribution experiment reports 0.1% NEH-relative gap at 1,000 jobs, while the ten-machine Gamma experiment has an approximately 4.6% average gap. Mean aggregation and batch normalization perform best in the listed component ablations. Appendix G's separate TSP model reports Gurobi-relative gaps 0.7%, 3.6% and 8.3% at 20/50/100 nodes; these are a different objective/reference from PFSS's NEH gaps. [pp. 11–12]

## Limitations

Imitating a heuristic does not certify optimal makespan. Appendix C explicitly shows NEH is worse than the reported MIP solution on eight-, nine- and ten-job examples. Scaling input shape to more jobs likewise does not guarantee solution quality, runtime or arbitrary-machine transfer. The evidence is limited to the listed distributions and benchmark subsets. Wilcoxon marks in Tables 1/7 concern the indicated paired comparisons at the five-percent level, not every performance statement. [pp. 5–7, 10–12]

The claim of linear test cost needs qualification. Table 3 leaves forward/backward costs as symbolic F/B factors. Those factors depend on job count: a fixed twenty-percent neighborhood still has quadratically many potential edges, and autoregressive attention examines remaining jobs across n decisions. The table is not an end-to-end proof of linear complexity including graph construction and attention. [pp. 3–4, 6–7]

The public scripts/configuration also differ from the printed default: the bundled checkpoint metadata lists 55 epochs, validation size 1280, learning-rate decay 1.0 and MAX local aggregation, while the paper describes fifty epochs, validation size 1000, decay 0.96 and an ablation favoring MEAN. Reproduction should identify the chosen configuration rather than silently treating every supplied default as the exact published experiment. [official scripts and checkpoint args.json]
