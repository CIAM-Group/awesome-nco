# Towards Realistic Earth-Observation Constellation Scheduling: Benchmark and Methodology

> **TL;DR:** AEOS-Bench provides simulated constellation scenarios and reference schedules; AEOS-Former combines learned constraint estimates, Transformer matching and iterative simulator rollouts. Its main advantage is the reported composite-score/energy tradeoff, rather than uniformly higher task completion or certified optimal schedules.

## Motivation

Constellation scheduling must handle moving satellites, arriving/expiring tasks, attitude maneuvers and battery/FOV limits. Simplified assignment environments can hide these interactions. The paper develops a common physics-based evaluation platform and a learned scheduler that can use current simulator states to choose assignments (PDF pp. 1–4).

## Contributions

The suite contains **3,907 satellite assets and 16,410 scenarios**, with 1–50 satellites, 50–300 imaging tasks and a 3,600-second horizon. Assets are tuned for stable simulated attitude control. Training uses 16,218 trajectories and 2,907 assets; three evaluation splits contain 64 scenarios each, distinguishing seen assets, 500 unseen simulated assets and 500 assets with web-sourced satellite properties (pp. 1–5, 22).

Reference assignments are generated by a distance-based initializer, iterative filtering and human review. The paper calls them ground truth, but the procedure does not certify globally optimal schedules. AEOS-Former is an additional scheduling method within this benchmark study, not a generalist checkpoint across unrelated CO problem families (pp. 4–7).

## Methodology

Basilisk models orbital dynamics, attitude control, power and sensors. High-level satellite-task assignments are translated into low-level pointing and sensor commands; the platform enforces dynamics, energy, FOV, continuity and time-window constraints and records successful observations. Tasks are point targets with required continuous observation durations of 15–60 seconds (pp. 4–5, 21–22).

The scheduler concatenates static attributes and queried dynamic states, adds temporal embeddings, encodes tasks and decodes satellite embeddings against them. A two-hidden-layer MLP predicts a completion-related logit and an attitude-control time. Its labels are approximate: a positive pair means the satellite contributed for a consecutive interval to a task completed somewhere in the reference trajectory. This is not an exact standalone test that the satellite can complete the task unaided (pp. 5–7).

Predicted constraint information biases cross-attention and filters action selection; a learned null-assignment embedding allows idling. Learned probabilities do not themselves guarantee feasibility. The hard validity checks belong to the simulator, and the printed multiplicative filtering rule in Eq. (8) should not be interpreted as a proof that all invalid candidates are excluded (pp. 5–7).

Training combines feasibility, timing and assignment losses. After supervised pretraining, new simulator rollouts exceeding a comprehensive-score threshold are added for retraining. The reported pipeline has three 30,000-iteration stages, rather than an RL convergence or optimality theorem (pp. 7–8).

## Experiments

Baselines are random assignment and adapted HAAL, REDA and MSCPO-SHCS. Six reported metrics cover completion (CR), partial completion (PCR), duration-weighted completion (WCR), turnaround time (TAT), sensor energy (PC) and a weighted comprehensive score (CS, lower is better). CS combines inverse weighted completion with time and energy penalties, so the preferred method can depend on those weights (pp. 8–9).

On val-unseen, AEOS-Former reports CS 4.43 versus MSCPO-SHCS 5.21, CR 35.42% versus 35.35%, and PC 68.99 versus 140.83 in the table's reported energy units. On the test split it reports CS 6.28 versus 7.33 and PC 40.91 versus 149.20, while **CR is slightly lower**, 19.25% versus 19.44%, and PCR is 22.31% versus 24.00%. The result is a completion-energy compromise, not dominance on every metric (p. 8).

Ablations likewise show a tradeoff. On val-unseen, iterative training alone reaches CR 40.88%, compared with 35.42% for the full model, but consumes roughly twice the reported sensor energy and has worse CS. The full model combines improved energy behavior with the selected aggregate objective. No repeated-run error bars are reported; the checklist explicitly answers No for statistical significance reporting (pp. 9, 16).

The experiment server has 256 CPU cores, 984 GB RAM and eight RTX4090 GPUs. Reported model training costs approximately 48 GPU-hours; evaluation uses 96 parallel simulator environments and takes about 30 minutes. This is an aggregate platform-evaluation cost, not a measured per-decision real-time latency guarantee (p. 8).

## Limitations

### Discussed in the Paper

Tasks are represented as points. Area observations, partial area coverage, flexible windows and spatial priorities are proposed future extensions. Scenario generation favors smaller constellations and more tasks because high-quality reference assignments are easier to obtain there (pp. 5, 21).

### Curator Notes

Web-sourced satellite properties still drive a simulator: the test split is not a real on-orbit execution trial. Approximate feasibility labels, heuristic reference schedules, selected CS weights and adapted baselines limit conclusions about general physical reliability or optimality. The text also says normalization statistics use the entire AEOS-Bench dataset; this should be distinguished from fitting preprocessing exclusively on the training split (pp. 2, 5–9).

**Metric units need reconciliation.** Tables 2–3 label TAT in hours yet report values around 6–7, exceeding the stated one-hour scenario horizon with releases/due times in 0–3,600 seconds. The current public TAT evaluator computes mean completion-minus-release in raw seconds; its sensor-energy evaluator sums assigned steps times sensor watts, without conversion to Wh at that stage. This audit therefore preserves the table numbers without inferring a corrected time unit or claiming independently validated physical energy totals. The exact conversions used for publication remain unresolved (pp. 2–3, 8–9, 22; author evaluator code).

The paper's MSCPO-SHCS citation [9] names a LEO-network QoS path-planning article rather than clearly identifying the observation-scheduling hill-climbing baseline described in the text. Baseline attribution/adaptation details and the reported composite improvement should be checked against the implementation before reproducing the comparison (pp. 3, 8–9, 11).

- Publication record: [NeurIPS 2025](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/7c40c5050bd029a3ea7ff8b01412f735-Abstract-Conference.html).
- Source text: [paper](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/7c40c5050bd029a3ea7ff8b01412f735-Paper-Conference.pdf).
- Implementation linked by the authors: [code](https://github.com/buaa-colalab/AEOSBench).

The current repository documents Python 3.11.10, PyTorch 2.6.0/CUDA12.4, Basilisk and [Hugging Face data](https://huggingface.co/datasets/MessianX/AEOS-dataset). Its training example/default uses 200,000 iterations, and the inspected current model forward loss is assignment cross-entropy; these should not be silently equated with the paper's three-stage 90,000-iteration, three-loss protocol. Source versions and omitted preparation steps must be recorded for replication.

This audit read the complete 22-page proceedings PDF, all 44 references, the checklist and Appendices A–C, and visually checked eight key pages. The publisher has no separate scientific supplement; OpenReview's two notes APIs returned 403. The complete 99-file repository tree was inventoried, including the orbit ZIP's 4,995 members, which contain no scientific PDF/video/ZIP supplement. Selected complete evaluator/model/config functions and the README were read; the full dataset, training and simulator were not executed.
